FRENCH NOTES FOR ENGLISH & KOREAN SPEAKERS

Volume 1

Weeks 1–2 · The French Sentence Skeleton

A high-yield, Toronto Notes-inspired course for converting scattered Duolingo and YouTube knowledge into usable spoken French.

MISSION
Build a compact working system for pronunciation, grammar, vocabulary and immediate sentence production. This is not an exhaustive grammar book; it is designed around conversational return-on-investment.
YOUR LANGUAGE ADVANTAGE
English gives you a large French vocabulary advantage through cognates. Korean gives useful conceptual bridges for politeness, negation, information structure and completed actions. We exploit genuine similarities without forcing false equivalences.

Volume 1 endpoint: introduce yourself, describe family and routine, ask basic questions, make requests, say what you have/want/can/must do, and talk about yesterday, today and tomorrow.

How to Use This Book

  1. Read once; retrieve repeatedly.
  2. Say everything aloud.
  3. Learn chunks: J'ai besoin de… is one useful unit.
  4. Use substitution drills.
  5. Translate less over time.
THE FRENCH ED95 PRINCIPLE
A small number of structures generate a huge number of everyday sentences. Volume 1 repeatedly trains être, avoir, aller, faire, vouloir, pouvoir, devoir, plus reusable sentence frames.

WEEK 1 — BUILD THE SENTENCE

1. Pronunciation Survival

You do not need perfect pronunciation before speaking. You do need enough awareness to avoid reading French as English.

FeatureWhat to noticeExamples
urounded front vowel; not English “oo”tu, rue
oucloser to “oo”vous, beaucoup
éclosed “ay”-like vowelété, café
rFrench uvular consonantParis, regarder
nasal vowelsvowel + nasal resonancebon, français
silent endingsmany final consonants are not pronouncedpetit, parlent
First pronunciation rule: don't pronounce every written letter.

Liaison

vous avez → approximately “voo-zah-vay”; les amis → approximately “lay-zah-mee”.

Don't master every liaison rule yet. Recognise common ones first.

6. Negation — ne … pas

subject + ne + verb + pas

Je suis fatigué. → Je ne suis pas fatigué.
J'ai le temps. → Je n'ai pas le temps.
Je comprends. → Je ne comprends pas.

KOREAN BRIDGE: conceptually compare with 안 / -지 않다, but French wraps the conjugated verb with ne … pas in careful speech.

Spoken French frequently drops ne: Je sais pas. / J'ai pas le temps.

14. Politeness — tu vs vous

tu = singular informal; vous = plural or formal singular. For travel and first interactions, vous is a safe default.

Think of vous as a social-register distinction that has some conceptual overlap with Korean politeness, not as a direct grammatical equivalent.

31. The Three-Tense Engine

PAST: Hier, j'ai travaillé.
PRESENT: Aujourd'hui, je travaille.
FUTURE: Demain, je vais travailler.
This is your first storytelling engine: completed action → j'ai + participe passé; current → present; planned → je vais + infinitive.

37. Where Korean Will Mislead You

FrenchKorean
Usually Subject–Verb–ObjectUsually Subject–Object–Verb
Articles matterNo article system
Nouns have grammatical genderNo grammatical noun gender
Subject pronouns usually explicitSubjects frequently omitted
Verb morphology is relatively compactVerb endings carry extensive tense/register information
Golden rule: use Korean to understand a concept quickly, but use French itself to learn the sentence.

38. Three-Language Strategy

French: Je dois travailler demain.
English: I have to work tomorrow.
Korean: 내일 일해야 해요.
Pattern: devoir + infinitive → ~해야 하다
French: J'ai besoin de…
English: I need…
Korean: ~이/가 필요하다
Rule: memorise avoir besoin de as a French chunk.
